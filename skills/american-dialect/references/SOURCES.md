# Sources and evidence map

Use these sources to justify linguistic features. Do not use a source for a claim it did not study.

## HDS — Vaux & Golder

**Vaux, Bert & Scott Golder. 2003. *The Harvard Dialect Survey*. Harvard University Linguistics Department.**

Current legacy results / successor survey:
https://survey.johndal.com/results/

What it supports:
- direct elicited lexical variation
- direct elicited pronunciation contrasts
- selected grammatical judgments
- geographic distributions of its survey answers

What it does not support:
- personality or politeness
- a holistic “voice”
- literary cadence
- stereotypes
- categorical claims that everyone in a location speaks one way

Later academic descriptions report that the online HDS contained 122 phonological, grammatical, and lexical items and received more than 47,000 responses in 2002–03. The results were not formally published as a standalone conventional paper.

## Written-language validation — Grieve, Asnaghi & Ruette

**Grieve, Jack, Costanza Asnaghi & Tom Ruette. 2013. “Site-restricted web searches for data collection in regional dialectology.” *American Speech* 88(4): 413–440.**
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1215/00031283-2691424

What it supports:
- regional lexical patterns can be measured in geographically localized written web/news text
- the authors compare mapped written variants with Harvard Dialect Survey distributions and report close alignment in most evaluated cases
- HDS-backed Midwest evidence for terms such as `gym shoes` can be treated as a real regional lexical tendency, not merely folklore

## Written American English — Grieve

**Grieve, Jack. 2016. *Regional Variation in Written American English*. Cambridge University Press.**
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139506137

What it supports:
- regional grammatical variation exists in written American English
- multivariate analysis can identify broad written dialect regions
- “Midwest” can be useful as a broad statistical written-language region, while still containing internal variation

Do not use this book as permission to invent a generic Midwestern style.

## North American dialect regions / phonology — Labov, Ash & Boberg

**Labov, William, Sharon Ash & Charles Boberg. 2006. *The Atlas of North American English: Phonetics, Phonology and Sound Change*. Mouton de Gruyter.**
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110167467

What it supports:
- modern dialect-region boundaries based on phonological systems
- Inland North / Northern Cities patterns
- Midland vs. North distinctions
- pronunciation evidence

Do not convert acoustic vowel research into comic orthography.

## Upper Midwest grammar — Spartz

**Spartz, John M. 2008. “Do you want to come with?: A cross-dialectal, multi-field, variationist investigation of with as particle selected by motion verbs in the Minnesota dialect of English.” PhD dissertation, Purdue University.**
Repository: https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI3344140/

What it supports:
- particle-like `with` after motion verbs, especially `come with`
- strong association with Minnesota / Upper Midwestern English
- informal syntactic regionalism

Additional university synthesis:
https://ygdp.yale.edu/phenomena/come-with

## Midland positive `anymore`

**Strelluf, Christopher. 2019. “Anymore, it’s on Twitter: positive-anymore, American regional dialects, and polarity-licensing in tweets.” *American Speech* 94(3): 313–351.**
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1215/00031283-7587883

What it supports:
- positive `anymore` is a distinctive Midland feature
- substantial intraregional variation
- lower production in western Midland cities such as Kansas City and St. Louis than in some eastern Midland locations

**Blanchette, Frances & Valerie Keppenne. 2024. “Anymore, this feature varies: An experimental study of non-polarity anymore.” *Proceedings of the Linguistic Society of America* 9(1).**
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3765/plsa.v9i1.5730

What it supports:
- modern experimental evidence for non-polarity / positive `anymore` as a Midland vernacular feature

HDS relevance:
- HDS questions 54–57 explicitly probe positive `anymore` judgments.

## Midland `needs washed`

**Wood, Jim, Josephine Holubkov, Ian Neidel & Kaija Gahm. 2022. “Some notes on the ‘needs washed’ construction.” *Yale Working Papers in Grammatical Diversity* 4(2): 1–19.**
Repository: https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/ygdp/13/

University research summary:
https://ygdp.yale.edu/phenomena/needs-washed

What it supports:
- core Midlands distribution
- particularly strong acceptance in areas including Indiana, Ohio, and western Pennsylvania
- rejection in much of the Upper Midwest, including Minnesota and Wisconsin

This is why the skill must not treat `needs washed` as a generic Midwest marker.

## Midland `wants in`

Yale Grammatical Diversity Project:
https://ygdp.yale.edu/phenomena/wants-in

What it supports:
- `want/need + directional preposition` constructions
- primary association with the Midland region
- casual regional usage such as `The cat wants in`

Use this as academic-project evidence unless a peer-reviewed source is added to the profile.

## Machine-readable HDS mirror

A useful open-source mirror/pipeline exists at:
https://github.com/tjstum/dialect

It reconstructs the original HDS state-level data and labels the original 122 questions separately from 26 later modeled questions.

Policy:
- use only question IDs 1–122 for this skill's HDS evidence
- never import the modeled 26 questions as if they were original academic survey data
- retain the HDS attribution/license when redistributing the underlying data

The mirror is a data convenience, not a replacement citation for Vaux & Golder.
